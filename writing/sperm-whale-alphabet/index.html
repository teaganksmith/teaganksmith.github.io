What makes a sperm whale alphabet

The a, e, i, o, u (and sometimes, why) of decoding a non-human language

Teagan Keely Smith · April 28, 2026 · Read on Substack →

Deep in the Caribbean, along the coast of the Island of Dominica, a sperm whale creates a massive noise. To human ears, it sounds like two clicks, and feels like a visceral vibration that shakes you in your dive suit. But to researchers at UC Berkeley, it sounds something remarkably like a vowel.

After collecting thousands of hours of recordings, a team at MIT and UC Berkeley analyzed sperm whale vocal clicks and found they have vowel-like acoustic patterns, qualities of language just like us. This brings science closer to uncovering how whales communicate with each other and how humans might eventually join that conversation.

How whales talk without vocal cords

Humans make sounds through vocal folds, which control the duration and pitch of noise. These noises then filter through the vocal tract, which shapes the frequencies into what we recognize as words. In a sperm whale, communication must travel through a much denser and highly pressurized water barrier compared to air on land. As a result, sperm whales evolved "phonic lips" located in the skull. These produce loud underwater vibrations that researchers termed clicks, which are organized into specific sequences called codas.

Can we distinguish an "a" from an "i" in the deep?

At UC Berkeley, researchers identified two distinct vowel types from these codas: the a-coda (F1 ~5,780 Hz) and the i-coda (F1 ~3,757 Hz). Like human a, e, o, u, y, these codas have specific acoustic differences, resonant frequencies at the peak of their noise with up to two peaks.

These sounds appear to be produced by design. They follow the "source-filter" theory, the same mechanism humans use to talk. While the phonic lips act as the source of the sound, the whale's internal air sacs act as a filter. By moving air between these sacs, the whale shapes the resonance of the click. The study suggests that whales actively control these "formants" to create specific, repeatable spectral shapes rather than just emitting random noise.

In human speech, speakers distinguish vowels based on the relationship between different frequency peaks. The i-coda features a second peak (F2), which gives the whales an extra "knob" to turn to change the meaning of their sound. This adds a new dimension to their potential vocabulary. Instead of simply changing the rhythm of their clicks, they can change the acoustic quality of the sound itself, much like the distinction between "beet" and "bat."

Social clicks vs. hunting pings

These clicks are distinct from echolocation. While whales use high-frequency clicks like a flashlight to find prey in the dark, codas are used for social interaction. Researchers observed that these vowel-like patterns appear specifically during social encounters at the surface, which suggests they are meant for communication rather than navigation.

So much of interspecies language research within the past 50 years has been focused on teaching apes human languages. So despite the progress in teaching chimps object names and sign language, these efforts to break the interspecies communication barrier were primarily attempted in English and Japanese. Other scientists, such as Gašper Beguš, advocate that science should instead focus on how these animals already speak to each other. By finding vowel-like structures in whales, researchers are finally meeting another species on their own linguistic terms.

"Codas are on many levels analogous to human vowels and diphthongs," the team noted in the study. They even found that whales use "coda diphthongs," where they slide from one vowel sound to another in a single breath, much like the "oy" sound in the word "boy." This discovery of diphthongs is particularly exciting because it suggests a level of motor control over the vocal tract that was previously thought to be unique to humans.

What training AI to listen reveals

To test these patterns, the team used a specialized AI model called a Generative Adversarial Network (fiwGAN). Researchers trained the computer to recognize the "vowel" signatures. Not only could the AI tell them apart, but it could predict which acoustic pattern a whale would use based on the social context. This suggests the whales follow a consistent set of rules for vocalizing.

Historically, biologists, linguists, and cognitive scientists believed humans were the only beings on Earth capable of creating a system of vowels complex enough for social communication. Finding these patterns in sperm whales suggests that the biological "hardware" for complex vocal signalling exists outside of the human species.

This paper reveals a layer of complexity that was previously invisible. Scientists used to think sperm whale communication was only the timing of clicks. But this research suggests that whales can keep the rhythm exactly the same while changing the vowel-like sound of every click, allowing them to pack more information into the same amount of time.

Can linguistics save the sperm whale?

Identifying these linguistic building blocks transforms how the world views whale conservation. Evidence that sperm whales possess a complex communication system provides powerful support for their cognitive sophistication. This data can serve as a tool for policy makers to establish stricter legal protections for whale habitats. By framing these animals as socially complex beings with a distinct social identity, scientists offer policy makers the specific evidence needed to argue for the expansion of protected "blue corridors" and marine sanctuaries.

Now that researchers have begun to identify the "alphabet", the next task is deciphering the information it carries. Future studies will focus on the context behind these sounds. Researchers are now looking to see if different pods have "dialects" or if these vowel-like sounds change based on the whale's activity. By linking these spectral patterns to specific interactions in the wild, researchers can begin to decode the social context of each conversation. This shift toward understanding the purpose behind the noise allows for a much more detailed map of how whale pods maintain their bonds and navigate their complex aquatic world.

A new window into marine intelligence

By learning to recognize acoustic signatures, this niche pocket of science is beginning to acknowledge a sophisticated intelligence that has navigated the deep for millions of years. This realization offers a vital tool for conservationists, turning the vibrations felt in a dive suit into a powerful argument for the continued survival of sperm whale populations.

Primary Source: Vowel- and Diphthong-Like Spectral Patterns in Sperm Whale Codas. Gašper Beguš, Ronald L. Sprouse, Andrej Leban, Miles Silva, and Shane Gero. Open Mind: Discoveries in Cognitive Science, 2025.
